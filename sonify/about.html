How it works

Turning the multi-probe microwave sky from the HalfDome simulations into sound and explorable maps — component by component, with every mapping written down as an equation.

In cosmology the angular power spectrum \(C_\ell\) is the central summary statistic: it measures how much structure the sky has at each angular scale, indexed by the multipole \(\ell\) (roughly an inverse angle, \(\theta\approx 180^\circ/\ell\)). The power spectrum of a sound is what sets its timbre, so the Sonifier maps the sky's spectrum onto an audio spectrum: large angular scales (small \(\ell\)) become low pitches, fine scales become treble, and the audio power per octave follows the sky's power per \(\ell\)-octave. The sky views then let you listen to the maps themselves, point by point.

The sky is a sum of physically distinct components, each with its own spectrum, frequency behaviour and spatial texture:

  • CMB lensed primordial light — the acoustic peaks; dominates large scales.
  • tSZ hot cluster gas scattering the CMB — a decrement below 217 GHz, an increment above.
  • CIB dusty star-forming galaxies — rises steeply to high frequency and high \(\ell\).
  • kSZ bulk motion of ionized gas — a faint, frequency-independent Doppler signal.
  • Total \(T_{\rm tot}=\) CMB \(+\) tSZ \(+\) CIB in each band. kSZ is not part of the Total.

The four pages

  • Power Spectrum Sonifier: hear \(D_\ell\) of any component or mix, in any band; sweep across scales and bands; play the spectra with instruments; hear two probes' cross-correlation as harmony (Duet); render reproducible WAV files.
  • Sky Explorer (flat full-sky map), 3D Globe and Inside the Lightcone: click or scan the sky to hear the temperature under the cursor, one voice per component, read from equal-area maps so that equal skies sound alike at every latitude (fully so once the full render replaces the provisional maps); massive clusters ring with a halo sound that encodes their mass and distance.

Design principles

  • Every audible feature either carries data or is labelled decorative. The full list is in What carries data. The Instruments Analysis preset has no decorative features; decorative modes (the Big Bang chord, the Musical preset, the artistic halo sound) stay available and are labelled “artistic” or “decorative” in the pages.
  • One convention per quantity. The plot and the audio use the same \(D_\ell\) array, the same \(\ell\to f\) map and the same level reference, and the paper states the same equations.
  • Physical by default, labelled when equalized. Levels are absolute and spectra beam-convolved (as observed) unless you choose otherwise; any per-selection rescaling is visible in the interface. The one exception is the Duet, whose two voices share one level by default so that the interval between them stays audible (why).
  • Deterministic. All randomness is seeded; Render WAV writes a lossless file and a JSON sidecar holding every setting, the seed and the code version.

From the sky to a spectrum

A temperature map on the sphere is expanded in spherical harmonics \(Y_{\ell m}\):

\[ \Delta T(\hat n)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell} a_{\ell m}\,Y_{\ell m}(\hat n),\qquad a_{\ell m}=\int \Delta T(\hat n)\,Y_{\ell m}^{*}(\hat n)\,d\Omega. \]

Statistical isotropy means all the two-point information lives in the angular power spectrum

\[ C_\ell=\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}\bigl|a_{\ell m}\bigr|^2,\qquad D_\ell\equiv\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2\pi}\,C_\ell . \]

\(D_\ell\) is the conventionally plotted quantity because it is the variance per logarithmic interval of \(\ell\):

\[ \sigma^2_{\rm sky}=\sum_{\ell}\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\,C_\ell\;\simeq\;\int D_\ell\;d\ln\ell . \]

This is the property the audio is built on: the sky's variance in a band of scales is the area under \(D_\ell\) against \(\ln\ell\), and the audio variance in the matching band of pitches is made proportional to it (spectral-density convention).

One spectrum array for the plot and the audio

All spectra come from one file, cl_data.json (version 2), on one grid of 400 multipoles from \(\ell=2\) to \(6000\): every integer up to \(\ell=85\), then logarithmically spaced (steps of 0.013 in \(\ln\ell\)). Bin edges sit at the geometric midpoints of neighbouring grid points, so every integer \(\ell\) in 2–6000 belongs to exactly one bin, and each grid value is the \((2\ell+1)\)-weighted mean of \(D_\ell\) over its bin. Between grid points \(D_\ell\) is taken as linear in \(\ln\ell\); with these edges the integral of that interpolant over the whole grid equals \(\sum_i D_i\,\Delta\ln\ell_i\) exactly, so every level the pages print is the same number the audio carries. The plot, the audio and the level readouts all read this one array.

Units are \(\mu\mathrm{K}^2\) in thermodynamic temperature. The HalfDome maps were transformed to \(a_{\ell m}\) up to \(\ell_{\max}=9000\); the audio uses \(\ell=2\)–\(6000\). The sky views use maps band-limited to \(\ell\le3000\) (Sky views).

The 2′ beam: observed or deconvolved

Every map is convolved with a Gaussian beam of \(\theta_{\rm FWHM}=2'\), as a telescope would see it. Its window is

\[ B_\ell^2=\exp\!\bigl[-\ell(\ell+1)\,\sigma_b^2\bigr],\qquad \sigma_b=\frac{\theta_{\rm FWHM}}{\sqrt{8\ln2}}, \]

binned on the same grid with the same \((2\ell+1)\) weights (beam_B2), so \(B_\ell^2=0.58\) at \(\ell=3000\) and \(0.11\) at \(\ell=6000\). The beam toggle in the Sonifier chooses between

  • observed (default): \(D_\ell\) as stored, beam-convolved; and
  • deconvolved: \(D_\ell/B_\ell^2\), the sky without the beam.

The plot and the sound follow the same setting. The choice matters at high \(\ell\): in the observed spectra the tSZ \(D_\ell\) peaks at \(\ell\approx1900\) and the CIB at \(\ell\approx3700\), but much of the CIB turnover is the beam. Deconvolved, the tSZ peaks near \(\ell\approx3500\) and the CIB keeps rising to \(\ell=6000\).

Pitch: three documented maps

The Sonifier uses three \(\ell\to\) pitch maps, each written as one function in sonify_core.js and printed in the interface: the analysis map, the musical map of the instrument voices and the Duet's own map. They serve different purposes and are never mixed within one sound.

Analysis map (Shaped Noise, Additive, Scale Layers, ℓ-Sweep, the plot)

\[ f(\ell)=f_{\min}\Bigl(\frac{\ell}{\ell_{\min}}\Bigr)^{\alpha},\qquad \alpha=\frac{\ln(f_{\max}/f_{\min})}{\ln(\ell_{\max}/\ell_{\min})}=\frac{\ln200}{\ln3000}=0.6618, \]

with \(\ell_{\min}=2,\ \ell_{\max}=6000,\ f_{\min}=40\,\mathrm{Hz},\ f_{\max}=8\,\mathrm{kHz}\), and the inverse \(\ell(f)=\ell_{\min}(f/f_{\min})^{1/\alpha}\). It is a straight line on log–log axes, so equal ratios of scale become equal musical intervals: one \(\ell\)-octave is \(\alpha=0.66\) of an audio octave (7.9 semitones), and the 11.6 \(\ell\)-octaves from 2 to 6000 span 7.6 audio octaves. Musical map (Instruments)

The instrument voices use a compressed law that spans five octaves over the same \(\ell\) range, starting on a note that identifies the observing band:

\[ f_{\rm mus}(\ell,\nu)=f_\nu\Bigl(\frac{\ell}{2}\Bigr)^{\beta},\qquad \beta=\frac{5\ln2}{\ln3000}=0.4329,\qquad f_\nu=110\,\mathrm{Hz}\times2^{s_\nu/12}. \]

Duet map (Duet)

The Duet plays its two voices on a narrower law of its own, three octaves over the same \(\ell\) range, the same in every band:

\[ f_{\rm duet}(\ell)=110\,\mathrm{Hz}\Bigl(\frac{\ell}{2}\Bigr)^{\gamma},\qquad \gamma=\frac{3\ln2}{\ln3000}=0.2597 . \]

This is the pitch of voice A (the lower voice); voice B sits an interval of at most an octave above it, so it never goes above 1760 Hz. Snapping (Snap voice A to the A-minor pentatonic scale in the Duet panel, on by default; the same setting as the Instruments checkbox) rounds voice A to the A-minor pentatonic scale as above; both ends of the range are A's, so the snapped pitch stays inside it. Voice B is always exactly the interval above voice A, so snapping keeps the interval ladder exact; with snapping off the Duet plays the continuous duet map.

Why a separate map, and why no band note? Until version 2.1 the Duet used the five-octave musical map, so voice A reached 7 kHz and voice B 14 kHz. At such pitches the instrument voices are nearly pure tones, and two pure tones a fifth or a tritone apart are both smooth once they are more than a critical band apart (Plomp & Levelt 1965), which leaves only harmonicity, a cue that depends on musical training (McDermott et al. 2010), to tell the fifth from the tritone; and the musical pitch of pure tones degrades above about 4–5 kHz (Semal & Demany 1990; Oxenham et al. 2011). With voice A at 110–880 Hz and the Duet's harmonic tone, the four intervals of the ladder stay well apart in roughness at every pitch the Duet plays (ladder). The musical map's band offsets (up to an octave for six bands) are dropped because they would leave two octaves or less for \(\ell\); the band is heard in the Duet through \(r_\ell\) itself (for example the tSZ's sign flip at 217 GHz), not through the pitch.

Why several maps? The analysis map is the one to compare against the plot: it is the same function that labels the plot's top axis. The musical map keeps the instrument voices in a comfortable register and makes the band audible as a note; the duet map keeps the Duet's intervals where they can be heard as consonant or dissonant. The plot's top axis switches to the musical map (unsnapped) in Instruments mode and to the duet map (voice A, unsnapped) in Duet mode. The artistic Big Bang chord uses a fourth, linear scaling (Synthesis), and the sky views use per-component registers instead of \(\ell\) (Sky views).

Spectral-density convention

Because \(D_\ell\) is the variance per \(\ln\ell\) and the analysis map gives \(d\ln f=\alpha\,d\ln\ell\), the synthesized audio has power per logarithmic frequency interval proportional to \(D_\ell\). Equivalently, its power spectral density per hertz is \(\propto D_\ell/f\):

\[ S(f)=g^2\,\frac{D_{\ell(f)}}{\alpha\,f}\quad\text{for } f(\ell_{\rm lo})\le f\le f(\ell_{\rm hi}),\ \text{else }0,\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \sigma^2_{\rm audio}=\int S\,df=g^2\!\int_{\ell_{\rm lo}}^{\ell_{\rm hi}}\!D_\ell\,d\ln\ell , \]

where \(g=L_{\rm ref}/\mathrm{REF}\) is the fixed level scale defined below. So the audio variance in any range of pitches is \(g^2\) times the sky variance in the matching range of scales. A flat \(D_\ell\) sounds like pink noise (equal power per octave).

Why not make the power per hertz \(\propto D_\ell\)? Under a logarithmic pitch map that convention adds a tilt of \(+3\) dB per audio octave (\(+23\) dB from 40 Hz to 8 kHz) and makes the small-scale components (tSZ, CIB) sound far too strong relative to the CMB. The unit tests check the convention directly: the power per \(\ell\)-octave of the synthesized buffers matches \(\int D_\ell\,d\ln\ell\) within 0.5% for several test spectra and the CMB, with no tilt.

Level: one reference, stated

Every analysis sound is scaled by one constant per family of displays, never by its own peak. The digital RMS of what you hear is

\[ \mathrm{RMS}=L_{\rm ref}\,\frac{A}{\mathrm{REF}},\qquad A=\sqrt{\int_{\ell_{\rm lo}}^{\ell_{\rm hi}}D_\ell\,d\ln\ell}\ \ (\mu\mathrm{K}),\qquad L_{\rm ref}=0.1\ (-20\ \mathrm{dBFS}), \]

so the audio RMS relative to \(L_{\rm ref}\) equals the sky-field RMS in the heard range (in \(\mu\)K) relative to REF, and the readouts give levels in dB re REF (0 dB re REF \(=-20\) dBFS RMS). Absolute and Equalized

  • Absolute (default): the references above, the same for every selection. A faint component sounds faint (kSZ is about 40 dB below the CMB).
  • Equalized (labelled in the interface): the same maxima taken only over the bands of the current selection (Play, sweeps) or of each voice (Instruments, and the Duet with Data levels). Levels are then not comparable between selections. Where a comparison is the point of the display, one reference is shared: Build-Up uses the Total's for all four stages, and the stereo modes use the whole selection's for both channels.
  • The Duet uses neither by default: its voices play at one common level (Duet levels). Absolute and Equalized apply to it only with Data levels.

Gain staging and the meter

The signal path is: players → data bus → monitor gain (−40 to +12 dB, default 0 dB) → safety limiter (threshold 0 dBFS, knee 0, ratio 20, so its make-up gain is exactly 1) → output. At 0 dB monitor gain the analysis modes peak near −6 dBFS and never reach the limiter. The meter taps the output after the limiter and shows dBFS RMS, peak, and dB re the active reference (= dBFS − monitor gain + 20). Renders contain neither the monitor gain nor the limiter.

Log volume (option, labelled)

A dB-linear compression for hearing faint structure, with its floor shown:

\[ m(a)=\max\Bigl(0,\;1+\frac{20\log_{10}a}{60}\Bigr),\qquad D_{\log}=D_{\rm REF}\;m\!\left(\sqrt{D_\ell/D_{\rm REF}}\right)^{2},\qquad D_{\rm REF}=\frac{\mathrm{REF}^2}{\ln3000}, \]

i.e. amplitudes from −60 dB to 0 dB re REF map linearly onto 0 to 1. For spectra it acts on the local density (\(D_{\rm REF}\) is the flat density whose full-range integral is REF\(^2\)), and REF is recomputed on the mapped spectra for headroom; for voices it acts on \(A_v/\mathrm{REF_{voice}}\). It is monotone in the data but not proportional to it.

Loudness compensation (option, labelled; approximate)

The ear is much less sensitive at low and very high frequencies, so equal sky power does not sound equally loud at 40 Hz and at 3 kHz. The loudness comp. toggle (off by default in the Sonifier) applies the inverse of the ISO 226:2003 equal-loudness contour at 60 phon, relative to 1 kHz:

\[ G(f)=\min\bigl[L_{60}(f)-L_{60}(1\,\mathrm{kHz}),\;30\ \mathrm{dB}\bigr],\qquad D_\ell\;\to\;D_\ell\,10^{\,G(f(\ell))/10}, \]

with \(L_{60}\) from ISO 226:2003 eq. (1) and Table 1, interpolated linearly in \(\log f\). The code implements this 2003 edition (its table of \(\alpha_f\), \(L_U\) and \(T_f\)), not the 2023 revision of the standard. It is a power weight on the synthesized density at \(f(\ell)\) (analysis modes) and an amplitude weight \(10^{G/20}\) on a voice at its pitch (Instruments: the musical map; Duet: each voice at its own pitch on the duet map, voice B's tone at its interval above the root and the noise cue at the root; with the Duet's common level the weight is taken relative to its largest value in the duet's register, +17.3 dB at 110 Hz, so that no voice exceeds the common level). Values: +30 dB at 40 Hz (capped; +34.2 uncapped), +18.6 at 100 Hz, 0 at 1 kHz, −3.6 at 3.15 kHz, +11.7 at 8 kHz. REF is recomputed on the compensated spectra (it becomes 897 μK, set by the boosted CMB at low \(\ell\)). The help box in the Sonifier plots the curve.

Loudness compensation equalizes perceived loudness only approximately: the contours are averages over otologically normal young adults, measured with pure tones, and are used here at one loudness level (60 phon); what you hear depends on your playback level, headphones or speakers, and hearing. It is a perceptual aid, not part of the data. The sky views apply the same contour to each voice's fundamental and to the halo sound of the clusters, and have it on by default (why).

Mixing components, with their cross-spectra

Selecting several components plays the spectrum of their summed sky, not the sum of their spectra. Components are correlated (the tSZ and CIB trace the same halos), so the mix includes the cross terms:

\[ D^{\rm mix}_\ell=\sum_{X}D^{X}_\ell+2\sum_{X<Y}r^{XY}_\ell\sqrt{D^{X}_\ell D^{Y}_\ell},\qquad r^{XY}_\ell=\frac{C^{XY}_\ell}{\sqrt{C^{XX}_\ell C^{YY}_\ell}} . \]

The correlation coefficients come from cross_data.json (71 bins in \(\ell\), per band for the frequency-dependent pairs), interpolated linearly in \(\ln\ell\) between bin centres at the current band and held constant beyond the ends. When cl_data.json carries the exact per-pair cross-spectra (Dl_cross, written by a full re-run of the pipeline), those are used instead. The plot draws the mix that is heard as a dashed white line labelled “mix (heard)”.

  • Total and its parts are mutually exclusive. The Total already is CMB + tSZ + CIB, so selecting Total deselects them, and selecting any of them deselects Total (the sky views follow the same rule).
  • kSZ is not in the Total. It mixes with anything through its own cross terms; Total + kSZ mixes through the Total's parts, \(D^{\rm Tot,kSZ}=\sum_{P\in\{\rm CMB,tSZ,CIB\}}D^{P,\rm kSZ}\).
  • Accuracy. With the current data, CMB + tSZ + CIB reproduces the Total's band power to better than \(10^{-4}\). Point by point on the grid the deviation is at most 0.6% at 143 GHz and up to 10% at 353 GHz (at \(\ell\approx80\)); it is zero-mean scatter, within 2.8 times the chance correlation of a single grid bin, because the binned \(r\) cannot follow this one realization's chance correlations within each bin. Without cross terms the error would be up to 13% at 143 GHz and 22% at 93 GHz.

Stereo modes

For Play with Shaped Noise, Additive or Scale Layers, the Stereo Mode puts different fields in the two ears:

  • Mono: the mix, centred.
  • Components L/R: the first half of the active components (in the order CMB, tSZ, CIB, kSZ, Total) left, the rest right.
  • lowest | highest band: the selection observed in the lowest band on the left and in the highest band on the right (the button shows the two bands).

The two channels carry the data's correlation between them, not an invented one. Each channel keeps its exact magnitude spectrum, and the right channel's phases are the left channel's plus a data-driven offset:

\[ \varphi^{R}_k=\varphi^{L}_k+s_k\arccos\rho(\ell_k),\qquad \rho(\ell)=\frac{\sum_{X\in L,\,Y\in R}r^{XY}_\ell\sqrt{D^X_\ell D^Y_\ell}}{\sqrt{D^{L}_\ell D^{R}_\ell}},\qquad s_k=\pm1\ \text{(seeded)}. \]

Every frequency bin then has interaural correlation \(\rho\); the mono fold-down \(L+R\) has the power of the combined field; and the random signs avoid a systematic delay between the ears. Across bands, CMB and kSZ are the same field in both channels, the tSZ (one Compton-\(y\) map times \(g(\nu)\)) carries the sign of \(g(\nu_1)g(\nu_2)\), and the CIB×CIB correlation between two bands is assumed to be 1 (an upper bound: it is not among the data products). The readout and the WAV sidecar state this assumption. Both channels use one level reference; the readout gives each channel's level.

CMB Lensed cosmic microwave background

Before recombination \((z\!\approx\!1100)\) the universe was an ionized photon–baryon plasma. Gravity pulled the fluid into dark-matter potential wells; radiation pressure pushed back. The result is acoustic oscillations: standing sound waves whose snapshot at last scattering imprints a series of harmonic peaks in \(C_\ell\).

The peak positions are set by the sound horizon \(r_s\) projected through the angular-diameter distance \(D_A\) to last scattering,

\[ \theta_s=\frac{r_s(z_*)}{D_A(z_*)},\qquad \ell_{\rm peak}\approx n\,\frac{\pi}{\theta_s}\ \ (n=1,2,\dots), \]

giving the first peak near \(\ell\approx220\). On small scales photon diffusion (Silk damping) suppresses power, so \(D_\ell\) falls beyond \(\ell\sim1500\). In this map the code finds the peaks at \(\ell=220,\,527,\,813,\,1125,\,1411\) (local maxima of the smoothed \(D_\ell\); Scale Layers), which the analysis map places at 0.90, 1.60, 2.13, 2.64 and 3.07 kHz.

Gravitational lensing

Intervening large-scale structure deflects CMB photons by \(\nabla\phi\) (the lensing potential), remapping the observed temperature:

\[ \widetilde{T}(\hat n)=T\!\bigl(\hat n+\nabla\phi(\hat n)\bigr)\approx T(\hat n)+\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla T+\tfrac12\nabla_i\phi\,\nabla_j\phi\,\nabla_i\nabla_j T+\cdots \]

Lensing slightly smooths the acoustic peaks, transfers power to small scales and makes the field mildly non-Gaussian. These are the dT_cmblens maps.

Spectrum: blackbody. In thermodynamic temperature units \(\Delta T\) is independent of observing frequency, so the CMB sounds and looks identical in every band and is stored once. Its RMS over \(\ell\) 2–6000 is 108 μK (−4.6 dB re REF). In the sky views it has the lowest register (146.8–261.6 Hz).

tSZ Thermal Sunyaev–Zel'dovich effect

CMB photons passing through the hot \((T_e\sim10^{7\text{–}8}\,\mathrm{K})\) electron gas in galaxy clusters are inverse-Compton up-scattered. The strength along a line of sight is the dimensionless Compton-\(y\) parameter:

\[ y=\int \frac{k_B T_e}{m_e c^2}\,n_e\,\sigma_T\,dl, \]

with \(n_e\) the electron density and \(\sigma_T\) the Thomson cross-section. The resulting temperature change has a distinctive frequency dependence:

\[ \frac{\Delta T(\nu)}{T_{\rm CMB}}=g(x)\,y,\qquad g(x)=x\coth\!\frac{x}{2}-4,\qquad x\equiv\frac{h\nu}{k_B T_{\rm CMB}}=\frac{\nu}{56.8\,\mathrm{GHz}}. \]

So \(g(x)<0\) (a decrement) below \(\sim\!217\,\)GHz, \(g=0\) at \(x\approx3.83\), and an increment above. For the bands here:

The tSZ power is dominated by massive clusters (a Poisson-like one-halo term); its amplitude is steeply sensitive to \(\sigma_8\) and cluster physics. In these maps the observed \(D_\ell\) peaks near \(\ell\approx1900\) (3.7 kHz on the analysis map); deconvolved from the beam it peaks near \(\ell\approx3500\).

The sign is audible in the sky views. Pitch follows the signed \(\Delta T\) (hot = high), so below 217 GHz clusters sound lower than the empty sky and above it higher. The tSZ image shows \(|\Delta T|\) (brighter = stronger) in every band, so eye and ear deliberately differ in sign; the note line under the controls says which applies at the current band.

CIB Cosmic infrared background

Roughly half of all starlight ever emitted was absorbed by dust and re-radiated in the infrared. Integrated over cosmic history, that dust glow from star-forming galaxies is the CIB. A single galaxy radiates roughly as a modified blackbody (greybody):

\[ S_\nu \propto \nu^{\beta_d}\,B_\nu(T_d),\qquad B_\nu(T_d)=\frac{2h\nu^3}{c^2}\frac{1}{e^{h\nu/k_BT_d}-1}, \]

with emissivity index \(\beta_d\approx1.5\text{–}2\) and dust temperature \(T_d\approx20\text{–}40\,\)K. Summed over redshift the CIB rises steeply toward sub-mm frequencies, which is why it is faint at 93 GHz and dominates by 353 GHz: its level rises by 36 dB across the bands here (−37.6 to −1.9 dB re REF).

Its anisotropy spectrum has two parts: galaxy clustering (a two-halo term, important at low \(\ell\)) and shot noise from discrete sources (flat in \(C_\ell\), so \(D_\ell\propto\ell^2\) at high \(\ell\)). The net \(D_\ell\) keeps climbing to small scales; the maximum of the observed spectrum near \(\ell\approx3700\) is mostly the 2′ beam turnover (beam).

Intensity → temperature

To put the CIB on the same thermodynamic-temperature footing as the CMB, an intensity fluctuation \(\Delta I_\nu\) is divided by the CMB's brightness–temperature derivative:

\[ \frac{dB_\nu}{dT}\bigg|_{T_{\rm CMB}}=\frac{2k_B\nu^2}{c^2}\,\frac{x^2e^{x}}{(e^{x}-1)^2},\qquad \Delta T=\frac{\Delta I_\nu}{dB_\nu/dT}. \]

These are the dT_cib maps (one per band, since both the SED and the conversion depend on \(\nu\)). The CIB map has a large positive monopole (e.g. a median of 6.1 μK against fluctuations of 0.86 μK at 143 GHz in the sky-view maps), which is why the sky views measure loudness from the empty-sky level, not from zero.

kSZ Kinetic Sunyaev–Zel'dovich effect

If the scattering electrons have a bulk peculiar velocity \(\mathbf v\) along the line of sight, the scattered CMB is Doppler-shifted. Unlike the tSZ this is a simple shift of the blackbody, so it is frequency-independent. In the usual convention (receding gas is cold)

\[ \frac{\Delta T}{T_{\rm CMB}}=-\int \frac{\mathbf v\cdot\hat n}{c}\,n_e\,\sigma_T\,dl=-\int \frac{v_r}{c}\,d\tau, \]

with optical depth element \(d\tau=n_e\sigma_T\,dl\). Because it is sourced by the product of density and velocity (the momentum field), the kSZ is small (\(\sim\mu\)K), peaks at high \(\ell\), and is hard to isolate. It is stored once as dT_ksz.

In the tools the kSZ is faint by design: −44.4 dB re REF, about 40 dB below the CMB. That is physically honest, not a bug; use Equalized to hear its shape.
Current data: the kSZ map in this release uses the opposite sign (\(\Delta T/T=+\tau v_r/c\), receding gas hot) and includes only halos with \(M_{200c}>10^{13}\,M_\odot\). The regenerated data will use the convention above, which flips the sign of every kSZ cross-correlation (the Duet's kSZ pairs). The kSZ×tSZ and kSZ×CIB correlations in this sky are real but specific to this realization: the halos around the observer have a net outward velocity at low redshift, a cosmic-variance feature that another realization could have with either sign. See Data.

Total The combined sky

At a given band the Total is the sum

\[ T_{\rm tot}(\nu)=\Delta T_{\rm CMB}+\Delta T_{\rm tSZ}(\nu)+\Delta T_{\rm CIB}(\nu)\qquad(\text{kSZ not included}). \]

Because the spherical-harmonic transform is linear, the Total's coefficients are the sum of the components',

\[ a_{\ell m}^{\rm tot}=a_{\ell m}^{\rm CMB}+a_{\ell m}^{\rm tSZ}+a_{\ell m}^{\rm CIB}, \]

so its spectrum carries the cross terms:

\[ C_\ell^{\rm tot}=C_\ell^{\rm CMB}+C_\ell^{\rm tSZ}+C_\ell^{\rm CIB}+2\bigl(C_\ell^{\rm CMB\times tSZ}+C_\ell^{\rm CMB\times CIB}+C_\ell^{\rm tSZ\times CIB}\bigr). \]

The pipeline forms the Total by summing the \(a_{\ell m}\) per band and then computing \(C_\ell\), so the cross terms are exact. They are not small: tSZ and CIB trace the same halos and are strongly correlated (\(r\approx-0.29\) at \(\ell=500\) at 143 GHz, \(+0.29\) at 353 GHz, the sign following the tSZ), and the CMB×CIB term of this one realization is not zero either. Mixing CMB + tSZ + CIB in the Sonifier uses these correlations (Mixing) and sounds like the Total.

Listening across bands you hear the CMB-dominated sky give way to a CIB-dominated one at high frequency and small scales: the Total is −4.6 dB re REF at 93–145 GHz and 0.0 dB at 353 GHz, where it defines REF.

Synthesis: turning \(D_\ell\) into sound

Shaped Noise, Additive and Scale Layers render the same \(D_\ell\) on the analysis map with the same level convention; they differ in texture. All three are computed in JavaScript as one loop of \(N=2^{19}\) samples (11.89 s at 44.1 kHz) by one inverse FFT, so each loop repeats seamlessly with no fades and no dips. All random phases come from the seeded generator.

Shaped Noise (the most faithful rendering)

True noise with the target spectrum: every FFT bin \(k\) of the loop (frequency \(f_k=k\,f_s/N\)) inside \([f(\ell_{\rm lo}),f(\ell_{\rm hi})]\) gets an amplitude set by the target density and a seeded random phase, and one inverse FFT sums them:

\[ x(t)=\sum_k A_k\cos(2\pi f_k t+\varphi_k),\qquad A_k=\sqrt{2\,S(f_k)\,\Delta f},\qquad \Delta f=\frac{f_s}{N},\qquad \varphi_k\sim U(0,2\pi)\ \text{(seeded)}, \]

with \(S(f)\) the target density, and nothing outside the range. Because the inverse FFT is circular, the buffer loops seamlessly. A phase is drawn for every bin whether or not it is used, so changing the range or the spectrum keeps the other bins' phases (which keeps crossfades between rebuilds coherent).

Additive

A bank of sine partials, one per 1/12 octave at nominal frequencies \(f_j^{\rm nom}=40\,\mathrm{Hz}\times2^{j/12}\), \(j=0\ldots91\) (92 partials; default), or one per \(\ell\)-grid point at \(f(\ell_i)\) (400 partials, as the offline script sonify_cosmo.py). Each carries the band power of its bin (the \(\ell\) range that maps into its 1/12 octave, or its grid bin):

\[ s(t)=\sum_j A_j\cos(2\pi f_j t+\varphi_j),\qquad A_j^2=2g^2\!\int_{\text{bin }j}\!D_\ell\,d\ln\ell,\qquad f_j=\frac{f_s}{N}\,\mathrm{round}\!\Bigl(\frac{N\,f_j^{\rm nom}}{f_s}\Bigr), \]

so each partial has a whole number of cycles per loop (seamless) and the total variance is the same as for the noise. Discrete peaks become audible pitches; with the 400-partial grid the top octaves are only 15 cents apart and sound noise-like.

Big Bang (artistic)

An homage to Cramer's “sound of the Big Bang”, labelled artistic in the interface. The CMB acoustic peaks are found in the data (local maxima of \(D_\ell\) in 150–2000: \(\ell=220,527,813,1125,1411\)) and played as a chord with Cramer-style linear scaling, \(f=60\,\mathrm{Hz}\times\ell/\ell_1\) (60, 143.7, 221.7, 306.8, 384.8 Hz), with amplitudes \(\sqrt{D_\ell/D_{\max}}\) (1.00, 0.65, 0.64, 0.44, 0.35). The peak multipoles and their relative amplitudes are data; the linear scaling preserves their near-harmonic ratios. The one-octave downward glide over 12 s, the detuned copies (±0.5%), the 35→20 Hz rumble and the reverb are decorative. Its level is set so that the held chord is at \(L_{\rm ref}\) RMS.

Crossfades and timing

Every rebuild (band, component, range or option change), mode change and stop uses an 80 ms crossfade, never an abrupt stop. Rebuilt loops start at the same loop offset as the buffer they replace, and the fade law keeps the power constant for any correlation \(r\) between the outgoing and incoming signal:

\[ a=\frac{1-p}{n},\quad b=\frac{p}{n},\quad n=\sqrt{(1-p)^2+p^2+2rp(1-p)}\qquad(p:0\to1), \]

linear for \(r=1\) and equal-power for \(r=0\). Every parameter that drives the audio (filter centres, gains, voice pitches, pans) is a function of the audio clock and is scheduled ahead (20 ms control steps, 0.15 s lookahead); the animation frame only draws.

In Instruments mode, Play (or ℓ-Sweep) sweeps \(\ell\) over the range, and every active component among CMB, tSZ, CIB and kSZ is a sustained voice. Duet: cross-correlation as harmony

Two probes \(X\) (voice A) and \(Y\) (voice B) play at once while \(\ell\) sweeps. Voice A sits at \(f_{\rm duet}(\ell)\) on the duet map (110–880 Hz in every band); voice B sits an interval above it. The interval and the stereo spread follow the scale-dependent correlation coefficient

\[ r_\ell=\frac{C_\ell^{XY}}{\sqrt{C_\ell^{XX}\,C_\ell^{YY}}}\in[-1,1], \]

from cross_data.json at the current band, interpolated linearly in \(\ln\ell\).

One harmonic tone for both voices

By default (Duet timbre: Harmonic tone) both voices play the same harmonic complex tone, sustained, with no body filters, vibrato or reverb:

\[ x(t)\propto\sum_{k=1}^{8}0.88^{\,k-1}\sin(2\pi k f t), \]

each voice calibrated to the RMS of its data level, like the instruments. With one timbre the two voices differ only in pitch, level and position, so what you hear between them is the interval; the probes are named by the selectors and the readout instead. The highest partial, \(8\times1760\) = 14.1 kHz, stays below the Nyquist frequency of any audio context running at 32 kHz or more. This is the timbre the ladder below was checked for.

The alternative, Instrument voices, plays the two components' instruments from the Instruments panel with the Musical preset (articulation, vibrato, reverb). It is labelled “Musical, not calibrated for dissonance”: with these timbres the rungs lie closer together in roughness (at the 880 Hz top, the octave–fifth and fifth–tritone gaps of the median instrument pair are 2 and 7 per cent of the largest minor-second roughness, against 12 and 21 per cent for the harmonic tone), and for some pairs the tritone is as rough as the minor second at the lowest pitches. (Until version 2.1 the Duet always used the instrument voices.)

The interval ladder

Intervals are ordered by sensory dissonance (roughness: Plomp & Levelt 1965, in Sethares' parametrization for harmonic tones), from consonant for \(r=+1\) to rough for \(r=-1\), in four equal bins of \(r\):

\[ i=\min\bigl(3,\ \lfloor 2(1-r)\rfloor\bigr)\quad\text{on}\quad[\text{octave},\ \text{perfect 5th},\ \text{tritone},\ \text{minor 2nd}]. \]
\(r\)\(r>0.5\)\(0<r\le0.5\)\(-0.5<r\le0\)\(r\le-0.5\)
interval (semitones)octave (12)perfect 5th (7)tritone (6)minor 2nd (1)

Positive correlations are consonant and negative ones dissonant, so the sign of a significant correlation is always audible. The order is checked, not assumed. For the Duet's harmonic tone, roughness rises from octave to fifth to tritone to minor second at every quarter tone of the 110–880 Hz register, with both of Sethares' amplitude rules and both of his parameter sets, and also (minimum rule) with voice B 3 to 40 dB below or 20 dB above voice A. Adjacent rungs stay apart by at least 12 per cent (octave–fifth), 21 per cent (fifth–tritone) and 37 per cent (tritone–minor second) of the largest minor-second roughness in the register; the smallest gaps are at the 880 Hz top. The same order is also the only four-rung equal-tempered ladder from octave to minor second that stays monotone for generic harmonic tones (5–8 harmonics, three roll-offs) at every quarter tone from 110 Hz to 7 kHz, and it agrees with the ranking by harmonicity (the fifth's partials nearly coincide with a harmonic series, the minor second's do not; McDermott et al. 2010).

Why only four rungs? In this register the harmonic tone would allow a longer monotone ladder (octave, fifth, major sixth, minor sixth, tritone, minor second), but every added rung splits a gap into smaller ones; longer ladders invert for the instrument voices (the five-rung octave, fifth, major sixth, tritone, minor second fails in about a fifth of the cases tested over instrument pairs and pitches) and, somewhere between 110 Hz and 7 kHz, for generic harmonic tones (that five-rung ladder above about 3 kHz); and four categories plus a neutral state are within the roughly five to seven categories that listeners can identify absolutely along one dimension (Miller 1956). The octave (not unison) stands for \(r\approx+1\) so that the two voices stay separable.

Significance gate

At low \(\ell\) a bin holds few modes, and a correlation of that size can arise by chance. The Duet uses the Gaussian estimate

\[ \sigma_r\simeq\frac{1-r^2}{\sqrt{N_{\rm modes}}},\qquad s=\mathrm{clamp}\Bigl(\frac{|r|}{\sigma_r}-2,\;0,\;1\Bigr), \]

with \(N_{\rm modes}=\sum(2\ell+1)\) over the bin (12, 16, 20, 24 in the four lowest bins; \(\sigma_r\approx0.004\) at \(\ell\approx500\)), interpolated log-log in \(\ell\). The weight \(s\) is 0 below \(2\sigma_r\) (gate closed) and 1 above \(3\sigma_r\). Voice B's tone and a band-pass noise cue share voice B's level \(L_B\) (the common level by default, or B's data level; Duet levels):

\[ \mathrm{RMS}_{\rm B\,tone}=\sqrt{s}\,L_B,\qquad \mathrm{RMS}_{\rm cue}=\sqrt{1-s}\,L_B , \]
  • for \(|r|<2\sigma_r\) (\(s=0\)) there is no voice B: the noise cue (Q = 4) takes its place, so you hear one tone and noise, and the readout says “not significant: one tone + noise cue”;
  • between \(2\sigma_r\) and \(3\sigma_r\) the interval fades in as the cue fades out (equal power at \(2.5\sigma_r\)), and the readout names the interval and gives the significance;
  • the cue is centred on voice A's pitch, so it carries no interval; tone and cue are uncorrelated, so their powers add to voice B's level at every \(s\);
  • the stereo spread is multiplied by \(s\), so the gated state sits in the centre.

The readout prints \(r\pm\sigma_r\), \(N_{\rm modes}\), and the interval or “not significant”.

Why noise and not a unison? Until version 2.1 the gated voice B fell back to a unison with voice A, 6 dB down, with a quieter noise cue. In the closest evaluated precedent, a sonification of the coherence between heart signals that maps low and high coherence to dissonant and consonant intervals (Frid et al. 2021), equal pitches mark the highest coherence; a listener who reads the ladder by consonance could take our unison for the strongest agreement. With voice B replaced by noise the gated state is not on the ladder at all.

\(\sigma_r\) assumes Gaussian fields with independent modes. For pairs dominated by a few massive clusters (anything with tSZ at low \(\ell\)) or by the observer's local velocity field (kSZ pairs) the true scatter between realizations is larger, so treat the gate as a minimum requirement. See the kSZ caveat.

Stereo spread and levels

Voice A is panned left and B right by \(\pm\,0.95\,\tfrac{1-r}{2}\,s\): correlated probes sit together in the centre, anti-correlated ones far apart. (The mono voices and the cue are boosted by \(\sqrt2\) to undo the pan law, so their power matches the dB-re-REF readout at any pan.) With 3D Spatial on, the voices also take HRTF azimuths \(\mp80^\circ\) times the spread.

Duet levels: one common level by default

The interval carries \(r\) only if both voices are heard. With levels that follow the data, one voice is often far below the other. Over all pairs and bands, voice B is more than 20 dB from voice A at 30 per cent (Absolute) and 48 per cent (Equalized) of the points where \(r\) is significant. In the default pair, CMB×tSZ at 143 GHz, \(r\) is significant only at \(\ell\le23\), and there voice B and its cue sit 47–59 dB below voice A (Absolute; 25–37 dB in Equalized), so the duet was one tone plus a voice that was probably masked (masking is not modelled here). So by default (Duet levels: Common level) voice A, voice B and the noise cue all play at one constant level, \(-5\) dB re REFvoice (\(-25\) dBFS RMS each), whatever the data. The Duet then carries \(r\) by the interval and the stereo spread only; loudness carries nothing. This is the highest whole-dB level at which the peak stays below that of the Equalized duet (\(-6.2\) dBFS) even if the crests of the two tones and of the cue coincide (worst case \(-6.4\) dBFS; the tone's crest factor is 8.8 dB, the cue's at most 14 dB with the default seed, 42, and seeds 1 and 7). The cue's crest depends on the seed: over 150 seeds it reaches 15.5 dB, and that worst case \(-5.6\) dBFS. Rendered duets peak between \(-8.2\) and \(-11.2\) dBFS with the default seed, and at \(-6.9\) dBFS in the worst of the other seeds we rendered. The decorative Instrument voices are not covered by this bound: their plucked and struck notes have larger crest factors, and two guitars (CIB×CIB at 353 GHz) peak at \(-1.2\) dBFS at the common level (\(-0.5\) dBFS with Equalized data levels). With loudness compensation on, each voice is weighted at its own pitch, relative to the largest weight in the register (loudness compensation), so the voices then differ in level by up to 8.5 dB (an octave at the 110 Hz bottom) while lying on one 60-phon contour, which equalizes their loudness only approximately.

Data levels, labelled “B may be inaudible where one component is much weaker”, make each voice follow its own \(A_v\) relative to REFvoice (Absolute) or to its own maximum (Equalized), as in Instruments mode; the readout then warns when the voices differ by more than 20 dB and suggests the common level. CMB and tSZ at 143 GHz, for example, differ by 34 dB at \(\ell=100\) and by more at larger scales.

Examples with the current data: tSZ×CIB at 143 GHz is \(r=-0.75\) at \(\ell=10\) (minor 2nd) and \(-0.29\pm0.004\) at \(\ell=500\) (tritone); at 353 GHz it is \(+0.20\) at \(\ell=2000\) (perfect 5th). Switching bands across 217 GHz flips the tSZ's sign and turns the dissonance into consonance.

What you hear: value maps in μK

Sound comes from value maps, not from the coloured images: one per component and band (tSZ, CIB and Total per band; CMB and kSZ once). They are equal-area HEALPix maps (Górski et al. 2005) at \(N_{\rm side}=512\), RING ordering: \(12N_{\rm side}^2=3\,145\,728\) pixels of 47.2 arcmin\(^2\) each (6.87′ across), in the same \((\theta,\phi)\) frame as the images. Each map is a 16-bit PNG of \(4N_{\rm side}\times3N_{\rm side}\) = 2048×1536 samples holding the pixels in RING order, decoded in the browser from the PNG bytes (no canvas read-back, which privacy protections can perturb) and converted exactly to μK with the map's stored range: \(v=v_{\min}+(v_{\max}-v_{\min})\,u/65535\).

A point takes the value of the pixel that contains it, found with the same ring-scheme ang2pix as HEALPix and healpy: the mean \(\Delta T\) over that pixel, with no interpolation and no smoothing. Values are clipped at the 0.05 and 99.95 percentiles of the pixels (equal areas, so these are fractions of the sky; the clipped fraction is stored). The maps are band-limited to \(\ell\le3000\) with a cosine taper from \(\ell=2500\), the same field the images show; with the 2′ beam and the pixel average the effective resolution is about 5′ FWHM. The tSZ, CIB and Total maps follow the selected band, so above 217 GHz the tSZ is an increment.

Why equal-area pixels? Loudness is measured in units of one \(\sigma\) per map, so equal skies must give equal values at every latitude. On an equirectangular grid the pixels narrow towards the poles, so their averages keep more small-scale power there: for a CIB-like sky the stored variance more than 60° from the equator (\(|90^\circ-\theta|>60^\circ\)) comes out about 1.4–1.5 times the global value, which would make the polar sky sound busier, give it larger pitch excursions and pile up the clipping at the poles. Smoothing the maps to about 15′ removes most of that but costs resolution (a point source centred in a pixel would lose about 7.5 dB). With equal-area pixels the variance in the latitude bands 0–30°, 30–60° and 60–90° (north and south) stays within about 3 per cent of the global value in tests with synthetic skies, without smoothing.

The images are for viewing only: CMB on the Planck CMB colour map, kSZ and Total red–blue diverging, the tSZ as \(|\Delta T|\) on magma, the CIB on inferno. When several components are overlaid, the diverging maps switch to dark-centred variants and are combined by per-channel maximum (a “lighten” blend), so each keeps its colour. A 1024×512 image loads first; the 4096×2048 image streams in for zoom.

Voices: pitch, loudness, register, timbre

Each active component is one voice with a fixed timbre in its own register. At a sky value \(\Delta T\):

\[ f=f_c\,2^{\,s/12},\qquad s=\mathrm{clip}\Bigl(5\,\frac{\Delta T-c}{S},\,-5,\,5\Bigr),\qquad a=\min\Bigl(1,\ \frac{|\Delta T-e|}{3\sigma}\Bigr), \]
  • Pitch = signed \(\Delta T\), hot = high: linear in semitones, \(\pm5\) semitones around the register centre \(f_c\) at \(\Delta T-c=\pm S\).
  • Loudness = \(|\Delta T-e|/\sigma\): amplitude grows linearly with the deviation from the empty-sky level \(e\) (the map median), reaching full scale at \(3\sigma\). Sparse signals (clusters, galaxies) are therefore quiet where the sky is empty. The empty-sky level matters because the tSZ and CIB maps carry a monopole much larger than their fluctuations (tSZ at 143 GHz: median −2.55 μK, \(\sigma=1.29\) μK).

Equal loudness (on by default here)

The ISO 226:2003 60-phon weighting is applied to each voice's fundamental, relative to the lowest register frequency (146.8 Hz), so every gain is \(\le1\): 0 to −6.4 dB in the CMB register, −8 to −12 (tSZ), −12 to −12.9 (kSZ), −10.9 to −16.2 dB (CIB). It is on by default in the sky views (off in the Sonifier) because here pitch and loudness carry two different quantities across four octaves: without it a 147 Hz CMB voice needs about 13.6 dB more sound pressure than a 2 kHz CIB voice to sound equally loud, which would confound the two. Turning it off applies a flat trim with the same median level. The halo sound of the clusters gets the same weighting (below). As in the Sonifier, the equalization is approximate (listener- and device-dependent).

Clicks, reference tone, mute and solo

A click plays each active voice for 0.45 s (15 ms attack), with onsets 40 ms apart in register order so that simultaneous voices are easier to separate. Ref tone (option) plays a pure sine at each register centre (the empty-sky level in the default scale, 0 μK in the physical scales) 0.12 s before each note, at 0.2 of full scale; while scanning it is a quiet drone. It is a reference, not data. Each component button has M (mute) and S (solo) for the audio only.

Scan: a moving ear

With Scan on, dragging (or the arrow keys) moves a continuous “ear”. Each component is one continuous oscillator whose frequency and gain glide (time constant 10 ms) at a fixed 40 Hz control rate on the audio clock. The drag path is resampled along great circles at a fixed angular spacing (5 screen pixels at the current zoom, at least half a value pixel), and one sample sounds per tick, so the ear moves at a constant 200 px/s and what you hear does not depend on how fast your hand moves. If you drag faster, the ear lags by up to 1.5 s and then skips ahead. The pending path is drawn as dots.

Galaxy clusters: the halo sound

The overlay shows the 3000 most massive halos of the HalfDome lightcone (\(M_{200c}\ge5.0\times10^{14}\,M_\odot/h\), \(\log_{10}M\) 14.70–15.41, \(z=0.02\)–1.62), dots sized by mass. Clicking near one, or scanning past it (once per cluster per 2 s, at most one per 150 ms), rings the halo sound, a bell-like tone (inharmonic partials 1, 2.756, 5.404). It is a parameterized auditory icon: an everyday sound, a struck bell, whose pitch and level are set by data (Gaver 1986; Brazil & Fernström 2011), rather than an earcon, an abstract motif whose meaning must be learned (McGookin & Brewster 2011). It carries:

\[ f_{\rm halo}=880\,\mathrm{Hz}\times2^{-30(\log_{10}M_{200c}-14.7)/12},\qquad \text{level}=\frac{500}{\max(\chi,500)},\qquad f_{\rm LP}=\max\Bigl[700,\ 9000\Bigl(\frac{500}{\max(\chi,500)}\Bigr)^{0.75}\Bigr]\,\mathrm{Hz}, \]
  • mass → pitch: heavier = lower (the size metaphor; magnitude-estimation studies find this negative polarity for size), −3 semitones per 0.1 dex: 875 Hz for the lightest to 257 Hz for the heaviest. The slope is fixed, not fitted to the catalogue.
  • distance → level, brightness and reverberance: the comoving distance \(\chi\) (Mpc/\(h\)) sets an inverse-distance level beyond 500 Mpc/\(h\), a low-pass that dulls distant clusters, and, with a constant reverb send, a direct-to-reverberant ratio that falls with distance. \(\chi\) comes from the halo redshift in the HalfDome cosmology (flat \(\Lambda\)CDM, \(h=0.6774\), \(\Omega_m=0.3089\)).
  • loudness compensation (with Equal loudness on, the default): the halo sound gets the voices' 60-phon weighting at its fundamental, relative to 261.6 Hz (C4, the pitch of \(10^{15.4}\)): \(+0.2\) dB for the heaviest clusters down to −7.2 dB for the lightest at 875 Hz. Without it a heavy, low-pitched cluster would need about 7 dB more sound pressure than a light one to sound equally loud (67.3 against 59.9 dB SPL on the contour), so mass would leak into the level that encodes distance. With it, clusters at the same distance sound about equally loud whatever their mass.

Picking uses a radius of 6 CSS pixels (14 on touch screens). On the flat map it is converted to an angle with the current zoom and compared by great-circle distance; on the globe and in the lightcone the visible markers are compared on screen. Hidden clusters (“Clusters: off”) are silent. The readout and the screen-reader announcement give \(\log_{10}M\), \(z\) and \(\chi\).

Artistic halo sound (off by default, labelled): a synthesized “hey!” or your own recorded clip (up to 3 s, stored only in your browser) replaces the bell. It is level-matched to the bell and still pitch-shifted by mass and given the same distance cue, but its timbre is decorative; being level-normalized, it gets no equal-loudness weighting.

Directional audio (globe and lightcone)

  • Globe: the listener follows the camera; click notes and halo sounds come from the clicked direction through an HRTF panner (use headphones), and the scan's panner follows the ear. Auto-spin turns the globe at one revolution per 65 s, independent of the frame rate. On the globe, Scan replaces drag-rotation.
  • Lightcone: drawn to scale (1 unit = 1 Gpc/\(h\)) in the HalfDome cosmology. You start at the observer (the origin); clusters sit at their comoving distance \(\chi\) (62–3182 Mpc/\(h\)) and the CMB shell at \(\chi_*=9418\) Mpc/\(h\) (last scattering, \(z_*=1091.9\), radiation included). Halo sounds are placed at the clusters' 3D positions with an inverse-distance model (reference 0.5 Gpc/\(h\)), so their level follows your actual distance and changes as you fly. Clicking the shell plays the CMB voice from that direction. Markers dim with distance from you.
  • The flat map is mono.

Coordinates

All positions are in the simulation's HEALPix frame \((\theta,\phi)\), not Galactic \((l,b)\). On the flat map \(\theta=0\) (the simulation's north pole) is at the top and \(\phi\) increases to the right (the mirror of the astronomical longitude-increases-left convention); on the globe north is up and \(\phi\) increases to the right seen from outside; from inside the lightcone the sky is the mirror image of the globe, as the real sky is. Readouts give \(\theta,\phi\) in degrees.

Keyboard and screen readers

The map and globe are focusable. Arrow keys move the ear (Shift for six times bigger steps; on the globe Up is north), Enter or Space plays the point, + and − zoom; with Scan on, the arrows extend the scan path. In the lightcone, I/J/K/L look around, W/A/S/D or the arrows fly, Q/E (or Shift/Space) move down/up, + and − change the field of view (10–100°), and Enter listens at the crosshair. A polite live region announces \(\theta\), \(\phi\), every component's value in μK and the nearest cluster after clicks and key moves (and, in the lightcone, the view direction and your distance from the observer).

Reproducibility

Seeded randomness

Every random number in the Sonifier comes from a seeded generator (mulberry32), with independent sub-streams derived from the seed and a name (noise phases, additive phases, layer noise, stereo signs, the flute's breath noise, the duet cue, the reverb impulse response). The seed is shown and editable (42 by default, as in the offline scripts). The same seed gives the same buffers bit for bit; a different seed gives a different but statistically identical realization of the sound. The sky views' reverb is seeded too.

Render WAV and the JSON sidecar

Render WAV builds the same audio graph as live playback inside an OfflineAudioContext (2 channels, 44.1 kHz), schedules its whole automation in advance, and encodes the result as 16- or 24-bit PCM (rounded to nearest, no dither; clipped samples are counted and reported). The length depends on the mode: Play renders exactly one seamless loop (\(2^{19}\) samples = 11.89 s, so the file loops too); ℓ-Sweep, Instruments and Duet one pass (twice for ping-pong); Build-Up, ν-Sweep and Big Bang their full length. The monitor gain and the safety limiter are not part of renders.

Next to the WAV the page downloads a JSON sidecar with:

  • the generator (page and core versions), time, mode and every setting, including the seed;
  • the audio parameters and the FNV-1a hash of the WAV bytes, the clipped-sample count and the peak;
  • the level convention and the REF values with what defines them;
  • the conventions in use: sweep window width, Butterworth order and carrier; the duet map, timbre, ladder and gate law (and whether the timbre is the one calibrated for dissonance); the stereo coherence model and its CIB assumption; the Build-Up reference;
  • FNV-1a hashes of cl_data.json and cross_data.json, their version and the band list;
  • the instrument calibration (Instruments, Duet), a determinism statement and the browser's user agent.

Determinism. The same settings, data and browser give a byte-identical WAV. The Shaped Noise, Additive and Scale Layers buffers are computed in JavaScript and are identical in every browser; oscillators, filters and the HRTF panner are rendered by the browser's audio engine, so other browsers (or versions) can differ in the last bits. Load settings reads a sidecar back, and Share links carry every setting, so a sound can be reproduced from either.

Code and tests

The pages are static: no backend and no build step. Audio uses the Web Audio API; the flat views use Canvas 2D; the globe and lightcone use three.js r128 from a CDN; this page loads MathJax from a CDN. All the mathematics is in two plain scripts with no DOM or audio dependencies, loaded by <script src> and testable in Node:

  • web/sonify_core.js (window.SonifyCore; a small public surface as window.SONIFY_API: ellToFreq, freqToEll, musicalFreq, duetFreq, buildTargetPSD, mixDl, duetInterval, duetGate, duetSigmaR, loudnessCompDb, iso226SPL, stereoCoherence, the three synthesizers, LevelModel, encodeWAV, mulberry32);
  • web/sky_core.js (window.SkyCore: PNG decoding, the HEALPix pixel lookup, geometry, picking, the scan path and every sky-view mapping) with web/sky_audio.js (the sky views' audio engine).

The tests run with plain node --test tests/*.test.js (Node 18 or later) and check, among other things: the pitch maps and their inverses; the spectral-density convention (power per \(\ell\)-octave of the synthesized buffers against \(\int D_\ell\,d\ln\ell\)); seamless loops; mixing CMB + tSZ + CIB against the Total; the duet map's range and the monotonicity of the duet ladder for the duet tone at every quarter tone of its register (and for generic harmonic tones over 110 Hz–7 kHz), the significance gate and its noise cue; the sweep window's edges and leakage; the ISO 226 contour against its table; deterministic buffer and WAV hashes; the PNG decoder, the HEALPix pixel lookup (against healpy) and the sky orientation; and smoke tests that run the real page scripts against a simulated DOM and Web Audio API.

Data provenance

Everything you hear and see comes from one realization (seed 100) of the HalfDome simulations (Bayer et al. 2025): FastPM N-body runs in a 3.75 Gpc/\(h\) box with \(6144^3\) particles and full-sky lightcones, in a flat \(\Lambda\)CDM cosmology with \(h=0.6774\), \(\Omega_m=0.3089\), \(\Omega_b=0.0486\), \(\sigma_8=0.8159\), \(n_s=0.9667\) (Planck 2015).

  • Maps. Lensed CMB temperature; tSZ from a halo-based Compton-\(y\) map (Battaglia et al. 2012 pressure profile) times \(g(x)\,T_{\rm CMB}\); CIB painted on the halos (Shang et al. 2012 model, XGPaint) per band and converted to thermodynamic \(\Delta T\); kSZ from the halos' optical depth and line-of-sight velocity. All in μK (thermodynamic), HEALPix \(N_{\rm side}=8192\), transformed to \(a_{\ell m}\) up to \(\ell_{\max}=9000\) and convolved with a 2′ FWHM Gaussian beam. Bands are treated as single frequencies (no bandpass integration).
  • Bands. 93 and 145 GHz (Simons Observatory mid-frequency), 225 and 280 GHz (SO high-frequency), 143 and 353 GHz (Planck). The pages read the band list from the data files, so adding bands needs no code change.
  • Total. \(T_{\rm tot}=\) CMB \(+\) tSZ \(+\) CIB, summed as \(a_{\ell m}\) per band. It excludes the kSZ.
  • Clusters. The 3000 most massive halos of the HalfDome lightcone halo catalogue by \(M_{200c}\) (in \(M_\odot/h\); a calibrated estimate from the friends-of-friends mass, not a measured spherical-overdensity mass), with the cosmological redshift (no peculiar-velocity term) and \(\chi\) computed in the HalfDome cosmology.
  • Raw data. The \(a_{\ell m}\) files (~810 MB each) are public at users.flatironinstitute.org/~abayer/halfdome-b16/; everything the pages need is in the repository.

Provisional data (November 2026).

  • Sky-view value maps are provisional: they are the earlier 8-bit, 21′ maps averaged onto the HEALPix pixels, so they hold no detail finer than 21′ and readouts are rounded to their level spacing; because the v1 cells narrow towards the poles, the polar sky still keeps more small-scale variance, the CIB most (at 143 GHz its fluctuation level is about 1.5 dB higher more than 60° from the equator than within 30° of it); and the CIB in every band but 143 GHz is the 143 GHz pattern rescaled to that band's mean and fluctuation level, so its structure is identical across bands by construction. The pages say so where it applies. The full 16-bit render (pixel means of the \(\ell\le3000\) field on the same \(N_{\rm side}=512\) pixels, smooth taper at \(\ell\) 2500–3000, no smoothing, ≈5′ effective resolution) will replace them without any code change.
  • The spectra and maps come from the June 2026 \(a_{\ell m}\) set, which is being regenerated. Its CIB comes from a run made before a halo-position unit fix (the corrected mean CIB intensity differs by −4% to +16% depending on band), and its kSZ uses the opposite sign to the usual convention and only halos with \(M_{200c}>10^{13}\,M_\odot\) (see kSZ). Levels, correlations and other numbers quoted on this page are from the current data and will change somewhat.
  • The cross_data.json bins currently follow the original binning (unweighted means, bins sharing their edge multipole); a re-run of the pipeline makes them half-open and \((2\ell+1)\)-weighted, consistent with cl_data.json.
